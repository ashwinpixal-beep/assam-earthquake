1 Abstract

After a major earthquake, engineers and relief agencies must decide which buildings to inspect first. We model the damage grade of 260,601 buildings surveyed after the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal, using 38 structural, legal and geographic features, and three ordinal classes: low damage, medium damage and near-complete destruction. A Categorical Naive Bayes baseline reaches 69.9% accuracy. Gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM and XGBoost) with engineered ratios and geographic density features raise accuracy to 75.7% and macro-F1 to 0.710 on a held-out 20% test split that was never used for tuning. The system also reports area-level summaries and an interactive predictor. These results are a statistical estimate from survey records and do not replace structural assessment.

2 Background and research question

Machine learning has been applied to post-earthquake building vulnerability and damage classification in several settings, including Nepal ([4]), the Indian subcontinent ([6]) and the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes in Turkey ([8]). Many studies compare a few classifiers on one dataset, and few connect building-level prediction to an interpretable area-level view. Naive Bayes is attractive for its transparency, but it assumes features are independent, which real building attributes are not.

Research question. How much does a modern gradient-boosted ensemble improve building damage grade prediction over an interpretable Naive Bayes baseline on the same survey data, and what limits the achievable accuracy?

3 Data

We use the training set of the DrivenData competition Richter's Predictor: Modeling Earthquake Damage [11], derived from the 2015 Nepal Earthquake Open Data Portal, compiled by the National Planning Commission with Kathmandu Living Labs [14]. Each row is one building. Features cover structure (floors, age, plinth area, height, foundation, roof and superstructure types), land and legal status, household counts, secondary use, and three nested anonymised geographic IDs. The labels are ordinal damage grades.

4.1 Feature engineering

  • Geographic density. For each geographic ID level, the share of training buildings in the same area (computed on the fitting slice only, so no label information leaks).
  • Structural ratios. Height per floor, plinth area per floor, a volume proxy (area × height), families per unit area, and age × floors.
  • Counts. Number of superstructure types and secondary uses recorded for each building.

4.2 Models

  • LightGBM [12]: multiclass gradient boosting with native categorical splits for the text columns and geographic IDs; 127 leaves, learning rate 0.05, L2 regularisation.
  • XGBoost [13]: histogram-based gradient boosting; geographic IDs as integer codes to avoid a memory blow-up from ~11,000 categories.
  • Ensemble. The final model averages the two probability vectors.
  • Baselines. Categorical Naive Bayes with the mid-review setup, and an improved Naive Bayes with mutual-information feature selection and tuned discretisation [1].

4.3 Validation protocol

The data are split once into 80% training and 20% test, stratified by grade. Inside the training part, a stratified 10% slice drives early stopping. The test split is scored once per model and was not used to choose any setting. All design choices (feature subsets, bins, smoothing, balancing) were selected with cross-validation on the training part only. The metric is macro-F1, which weights the rare grade 1 equally, and accuracy, which equals the competition's micro-F1.

How the result compares with the target and literature

The original goal was 85% accuracy. We did not reach that level, and we do not expect to on this survey without other information. Strong public entries in the DrivenData competition score roughly 0.74–0.76 micro-F1 [11], which is the same as accuracy here. Reaching 85% would most likely require information that the survey does not contain; a higher figure reported on this data would need checking for leakage between training and test rows.

The base paper P4 [4] reports macro-F1 0.729 on Nepal survey data with SMOTE-XGBoost, under its own split and preprocessing. Our final ensemble reaches macro-F1 0.710 on a separate protocol. The two numbers are close but not directly comparable.

8 Limitations and ethical use

  • Statistical, not structural. Predictions are estimates from 2015 survey records. They do not replace inspection by a qualified engineer.
  • Single dataset and period. The model reflects one earthquake, one survey and the construction practices of that time.
  • Imbalance. Grade 1 is the rarest class (9.6%) and remains the hardest to predict (F1 about 0.62).
  • No location coordinates. Geographic IDs are anonymised, so risk cannot yet be placed on real districts or 1-acre plots.
  • Single split. Results come from one stratified split. Differences below about 0.5 points between models should not be over-interpreted.
  • Responsible use. Outputs should inform prioritisation, never replace structural judgement. The site collects no personal data.

9 Reproducibility

All code and metrics are included. Training uses the competition's train_values.csv and train_labels.csv.

# 1. train the final model and write metrics (about 3 minutes on 2 CPUs)
python model.py train --values train_values.csv --labels train_labels.csv --out models

# 2. baselines and boosting comparison
python damage_nb_compare.py --values train_values.csv --labels train_labels.csv --out results
python damage_boost.py      --values train_values.csv --labels train_labels.csv --out results_boost

# 3. regenerate site data and run the web app
python web/build_results.py
python web/build_site_data.py --values train_values.csv --labels train_labels.csv
uvicorn web.app:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8010
